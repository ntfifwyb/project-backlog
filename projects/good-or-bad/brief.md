# Good or Bad

## Summary *

A "classifier" app where the human is the classifier. The app shows one image at a time
(donuts, a salad, a hobby, an activity) and the person labels it **GOOD** or **BAD**.
Context does not matter: donuts are BAD, salad is GOOD, full stop. The point is for people
to learn what they're putting into their bodies, and the same idea applies beyond food, to
hobbies and other things.

## Problem *

People excuse choices with context ("donuts are good in context, for instance if you're
spending time with someone"). The app rejects that: each item is judged on its own, so
people learn what the thing itself does to them.

## Users and context

Public: anyone can use it. Runs as a phone-friendly web page.

## Scope *

### Must (MVP)

- Show one image at a time; the person labels it GOOD or BAD.
- Items cover food first, but also hobbies and other things.
- Labels ignore context: each item has one answer, not "it depends".
- After labelling, reveal the answer with a **long** why: everything that's bad (or good)
  about it, including side effects down the chain (donuts → sugar → acne, overweight,
  blood pressure rise).
- Claude generates the items and their answers.
- Answers are as objective as possible: each item is researched and gets an absolute
  GOOD or BAD, no in-between. Rule of thumb from the user: if it is avoidable, it is
  generally bad. This includes medicine (e.g. paracetamol: good or bad?).

### Not doing

- Context-dependent answers ("good if you're with friends").

## Constraints

- Anthropic Usage Policy verdict (read 2026-11-12, AUP effective 2026-11-12): **allowed
  with limits**. Generic wellness/education content is excluded from the high-risk medical
  requirements, as long as labels aren't applied to the individual's own conditions,
  symptoms or medications. Limits: health claims must be accurate (no health
  misinformation); no body-weight or body-shape framing and nothing that encourages
  restriction, purging or compensatory exercise; if Claude talks to users, disclose it's
  AI; any stored per-user labels are health data (consent, clear data policy). Recheck if
  the app personalizes by health condition or is aimed at minors. Now that it's public and
  includes medicine, the accuracy and body-image limits matter most: explanations can
  name health effects such as weight gain as facts, never as judgements of people's
  bodies.

## Decided

- The human does the labelling; the app has a fixed GOOD/BAD answer per item, independent
  of context, with no in-between.
- Platform: web page, phone-friendly.
- Audience: public, anyone.
- Items and answers: generated by Claude, researched per item.

## Open questions *

- [x] What happens after a label? → Reveal the answer plus a long explanation, including
  side effects.
- [x] Who sets the answers? → Claude generates items and answers.
- [x] Where does it run? → Web page, phone-friendly.
- [x] Who is it for? → Public, anyone.
- [ ] Medicine items: an absolute BAD on a medicine (e.g. paracetamol) can read as "don't
  take it", which is a health-misinformation risk under the policy. Options: (a) keep
  medicines, but every medicine explanation says when it's needed and not to stop
  prescribed medicine; (b) leave medicines out.
- [ ] How are Claude's answers checked before they go live (policy: health claims must be
  accurate)? Options: (a) each item cites its sources and the user reviews it before
  publishing; (b) generated at build time with sources, published without manual review.

## Risks and unknowns

- Who decides the "right" answer for borderline items, and on what source, so labels stay
  accurate (see the policy limits above).
